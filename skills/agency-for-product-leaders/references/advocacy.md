# Part II (c): Advocacy

Use this file when the person wants a promotion, raise, or resources; says yes to everything; has a team whose work isn't seen; or needs to push back on goals handed down from above. It covers chapter 11 of *Agency* and the book's conclusion.

## 1. Why advocacy matters

Visibility is not understanding. Others can see your team's outcomes and still miss the context, trade-offs, and effort behind them, and leaders rarely have ground-level context. Advocacy bridges strategic intent and operational reality, and it's a sign of seniority.

**Cost of not advocating for the team:** drive-by goal setting (goals handed down with little input, and the PM seen as unable to challenge scope constructively), teammates struggling unseen, low morale, and disengagement.
**Cost of not advocating for yourself:** others' assumptions define you, you get typecast, you miss resources and collaboration, your impact goes unrecognized, and you lose advancement opportunities.

---

## 2. Advocacy for others

**Commit with context: the "informed yes."** Saying yes on behalf of your team without their input erodes their confidence in you. An informed yes means you and your team share an understanding of the aim, purpose, risks, and challenges before committing. Watch for: ambiguity about your purview and when to delegate or decline; saying yes out of fear of seeming not a team player; uncertainty about your authority to push back on senior requests; and leaders who don't understand what a change takes to build.

**Avoid drive-by goal setting.** Don't take goals from senior leaders, stay silent, commit your team, and forward the goals downstream. It diminishes trust in your advocacy, excludes the people who can judge feasibility, signals their insight isn't valued, and lowers accountability later. Leaders often hold the "why" and the team holds the "how"; the two only connect when brought together. Share the vision and desired user outcome, ask the team about concerns, and learn whether they have the tools, time, and information. Weigh **capacity and cognitive load**: too many or oversized goals lead to partly finishing many things and delivering none. The balance: some decisions need the team's voice early, others need your synthesis first. Make commitments *with* the team, not *for* it.

**Inspire and resonate; don't just ask for help.** The author's team on mobile was repeatedly asking stakeholders to "be an advocate for us," and it didn't land because the "so what" was missing. What worked: show how mobile work affects delivery speed and quality; share bite-sized facts and trends on mobile usage and business impact; run sessions that let QA help test; elaborate on the team's complexities when others discuss feedback on their reports; ask leaders to amplify the message; build knowledge-sharing among engineers. Principles: understand the knowledge others have and the pressures they face; be your team's megaphone; share relevant information consistently, not reactively; ask senior leaders to amplify key messages; aim for people to care, not just comply. Meet people where they are and tell compelling stories tied to how it matters to them and the business.

**Acknowledgment and well-being.** Make clear that well-being, inside and outside work, is part of how goals and expectations are set. Encourage and celebrate time off; check in on life outside work; share resources. Speak up when others misrepresent or disadvantage a teammate. Publicly acknowledge contributions (the engineer whose process sped everyone up, the designer who simplified the flow, the marketer behind a successful go-to-market). If there's no channel for recognition, create one.

**Compound career support and accountability.** Understand each person's aspirations and advocate for their growth. You see their work differently from their formal manager. Hold regular one-on-ones that include performance and goals; ask what they hope to accomplish and what steps they're considering; collaborate with their manager so support comes from several angles; encourage self-advocacy. When someone makes a mistake, stay engaged: surface missing context, acknowledge your part, and work out what to do now and what to adjust. Standing with people in the middle of a storm builds a different kind of trust.

## 3. Advocacy for you

**Make impact seen and understood.** The image: you swim away from a boat, lose sight of it, and wait to be noticed. No one comes, and the boat drifts. Don't assume the right people will just know. You are both the author and the narrator of your story. Self-advocacy isn't overstepping or demanding; done with purpose, empathy, and authenticity, it connects what matters to you with what the business needs. Many people avoid it for fear of bragging; ask them what they'd tell a friend who wouldn't share wins before a promotion conversation.

**Support in your current role.** First use the skills, tools, and resources already available. Then, before asking decision-makers for training, tools, or headcount, prepare answers to these six questions (and share your thinking even if you can't answer them all):
1. Have I exhausted the skills, tools, and resources available to me?
2. Are there low- or no-cost options?
3. By when does the support need to be in place to matter most?
4. Is there a trial, pilot, or abbreviated version to test efficacy first?
5. What is the expected ROI (financial or otherwise) and in what time frame? If hard to quantify, what is the risk of not acting?
6. Who will track outcomes and report on cost, usage, and value?
Constraints differ: cost in lean companies, approvals and bureaucracy in large ones.

**Career growth.** Clarify the strengths that define your value today, the capabilities you want to grow next, and how both help the business. Develop your storytelling about impact. Be explicit about direction so you aren't typecast (e.g., "I don't want to be the project-management PM"; I want product-growth work). Managers can't support a direction they don't know exists. Tips for these conversations:
1. **Keep checking in with yourself**: what has changed since the last conversation (enjoyment, skills, vision)?
2. **Make impact part of regular one-on-ones**: short lists of discussion points on user impact, company finances, and other benefits (team morale and retention, process improvements that raised velocity). This avoids saving everything for the annual review.
3. **Give your manager a heads-up.** Surprises are for birthdays; send context in advance so they can prepare. If asking for a raise, share achievements before the meeting.
4. **If no process exists, create one.** The author drafted her own review doc with KPIs aligned to her role and organizational needs and asked her manager whether she'd support her moving to the next level if she met them.

**Action plans.** Leave each conversation with a shared understanding of options, timeline, and next steps; otherwise you loop through the same conversation. If your manager doesn't initiate, you do: "In our next one-on-one, can we talk about what would need to change in my performance to be on track for the next step? I can draft some ideas and actions to review." Agree on a realistic timeline and recurring check-ins. If you want a change (new industry, more technical, IC vs. manager), start carving out weekly time to research it and talk to people who've made the move.

**When the answer is no.** Sometimes a manager rejects or ignores a plan. That is information, not a failure; it removes ambiguity about what the environment will support. In the book's example, the PM took the weekend to think, consulted a trusted colleague about the most professional path, and left gracefully with good references. No decision is final; you can always choose again. Offer this when the person describes an environment that stays unsupportive after they've raised the issue (see the PRD in `alignment.md`).

**Exercises to offer:** *Team:* Where have you said yes for your team without their input? Whose contribution could you acknowledge this week? *Career:* What do you want to be known for? What impact will you add to your next one-on-one? What would you draft for a growth plan?

## 4. Putting it together: the one-thing-this-week close

The book's conclusion distills the two halves: alignment (self-awareness, values and purpose, life-first career design, imposter patterns, an empowered mindset) lets agency take root; action (altitude, dropping the knowledge fallacy, catching burnout early, loosening early attachment, intentional communication, advocacy) makes it visible. The goal isn't to change overnight. **Progress is one step, learned from, then the next.**

End coaching with one concrete practice for this week, not ten. For example: protect one block for purposeful focus; identify one assumption that needs updating; prepare one meeting with intention instead of urgency; advocate for one teammate or for yourself.
