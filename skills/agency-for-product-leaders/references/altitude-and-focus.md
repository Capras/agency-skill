## 1. Altitude

**The core idea.** Execution earns credibility; perspective expands influence. A PM can have a productive day clearing tickets and still be seen as stuck, because senior leaders think in *leverage*: Did we move the most important initiative forward? Did this decision bring us closer to the outcomes we care about? If influence feels limited or promotions aren't being discussed, the problem is often not effort but **altitude**: staying too close to the ground for too long.

**Gain vantage points through empathy.** Empathy lets you see beyond your own scope, the way aerial imagery shows a whole town. Without it, PMs miss market and competitor shifts, optimize only for users and overlook business opportunity (e.g., a free feature with no revenue thinking), miss efficiency gains, and ignore cross-team dependencies. Three types (from Ekman and Goleman):
- **Cognitive empathy**: put yourself in someone's shoes even if you don't share their feelings. Use a defensive reaction as the cue to pause and ask how the situation looks from their side.
- **Emotional empathy**: feel what they feel; builds rapport and leads to outreach and support.
- **Compassionate empathy**: moves from understanding to helping.
The reframe to aim for: from "I don't understand why they made that decision" to "Help me understand how you're thinking about this."

**See the layers of the system.** Ask which layer the person understands best and which needs attention:
- **You**: know your learning style; focus on one or two growth areas at a time; give yourself a benchmark; let the right person lead.
- **The team**: understand individual motivations and success criteria; keep communication loops going: context, reiteration, clarity, outcome, repeat.
- **The product**: get good at defining the problem; drive urgency; hone product sense.
- **The business**: understand company finances; tie business needs to product goals; connect the dots across functions.
- **Externalities**: market, competitors, technology, customers. Make keeping up easy on yourself, experiment with new technology, and talk to customers.

**Act with altitude** (five practices):
1. **Be the driver.** Heavy top-down direction can turn a PM into a spectator who executes and never builds the muscles to ask strategic questions. Don't treat every deadline as sacred because it exists; sometimes urgency is real, sometimes it's inherited. A PM's job is to say what leaders need to hear, not only what they want to hear.
2. **Speak up when something isn't working.** If a request doesn't make sense to you, it probably doesn't to others. Ask the leader to explain their thinking. Don't rely on their reaction to validate your input; a muted reaction doesn't mean the idea was wrong.
3. **Challenge solution-first thinking.** If a group can't give feedback on an idea, they may not know what problem it solves. Define the customer problem first; it makes testing faster.
4. **Look for more doors.** When offered one or two options, ask what doors three, four, or five look like. A reactive PM executes; a strategic PM asks whether it's the right problem. Considering several options at once tends to produce faster and better decisions: it clarifies the landscape, reduces attachment to any one idea, and gives a built-in fallback. Try: "Why don't we try…?" or "Based on the data you shared, another option could be…"
5. **Aim for speed to value over product precision.** Watch for low-leverage polishing: doing others' go-to-market tasks, tweaking small design details instead of core UX issues, delaying a release for one more check, debating the "best" approach instead of committing and learning. Balance against risk tolerance (a startup and a regulated enterprise differ).

**Exercises to offer:** Where am I operating too close to the ground? Which layer needs my attention? When did empathy last help me understand a frustrating colleague? What decision would benefit from "what other doors are available?" Where am I overvaluing precision over learning?

## 2. The knowledge fallacy and the generative process

**The myth of the all-knowing PM.** Many PMs feel pressure to know every ticket, bug, and detail, and to produce a high volume of work. But as responsibilities grow, you can't hold pixel-level detail on everything, and shouldn't try. The shift is from knowing everything to focusing on the right things: use your mind for thinking, not storage; filter signal from noise (including the noise in your own head). Productivity is about impact, not volume.

**Redefine responsibilities and priorities.**
- Ask: "If I achieved nothing else this week/quarter, what single accomplishment would most improve the product for users and the company and move me toward my goals?" Then ask: "Would my manager's answer match mine?" Check in rather than risk drift.
- If overwhelmed, it's usually because expectations are unclear or you aren't saying no enough.
- **"Will it make the boat go faster?"** was the British rowing team's guiding question. The author's refinement: many things make the boat go faster, so ask **what would make the boat go fastest?** Which lever has the biggest impact? If facing one large priority, use the same question to find the first piece that moves you fastest.

**Give clear direction** so the team doesn't need you to hold everything. Teammates should always understand: the **impact** (why), the **goal** (the destination), **expectations** (what's expected of them at each stage, and what they can expect from you), **change awareness** (how to operate when the unexpected arises), and **decisive action** (making timely, informed decisions). Define these collaboratively; the PM's job is alignment, clarity, and visibility, through repeated verbal and written communication.

**Empowered empathy.** PMs over-take work for two reasons: *preservation* (protecting progress, your image as capable, relationships) and *misplaced empathy* ("Happy to help!" without learning what support the person actually wants). Redirect empathy to what people want: to contribute meaningfully and be recognized. Before taking a task in someone else's domain, ask: "Would they be faster, better, or more interested in doing this as a growth opportunity?" If yes, ask them to take it, with a date, the rationale for the timeline, and the impact of doing or not doing it. Sample phrasing: ask whether they have capacity; say "I know that's your domain, but I'd like to help; what support do you need from me?"; propose times rather than leaving a deadline open.

**The generative process: scan, synthesize, share.** An ongoing cycle of gathering information, connecting insights, and turning them into action. Range (learning across disciplines) and repetition both strengthen it.
- **Scan.** Quickly identify the people and information that will help solve a problem. Core elements: the problem or goal; who is needed; what information and resources you need; what could get in the way. Discovery questions: What do I need to find out to better serve the customer and the business? Who and what sources will clarify the problem fastest (customers, prospects, analytics tools, analysts, other PMs, support, engineers)? What questions will reveal pain points (watch a customer do the task; ask how they do it today)? How do I help others understand the problem and its business impact (write an **impact summary** with numbers)? Who will give honest input on whether we're asking the right questions? Writing the answers down strengthens your mental scanning.
- **Synthesize.** Don't rely on execution alone; build perception and synthesis. Method: (1) **document observations**; (2) **identify motifs** (recurring patterns, sentiments, or absences; listen for what is *not* said); (3) **outline implications and insights** for each motif. Note where confidence is thin (small sample, how a question was framed) and follow up or test before moving.
- **Share.** The goal isn't to show everything you found; it's to tell the story of your users in a way that focuses and energizes people. Use narrative frames: "Imagine you're a [user] and you've just done [X]…". Tailor to what the audience doesn't yet know: big picture, user insights, updates since last time. Link user goals to business goals.

**Exercises to offer:** Where are you unclear on your responsibilities? What would you drop if the boat had to go fastest? Which task in someone else's domain could you hand back with a date and context?

## 3. The slow burn: burnout

Burnout builds quietly, like driving with the gas light on. Contributors: lack of resources, support, and autonomy to address stressors (can't speak up about poor decisions, pulled away from core work with no support to deprioritize, an unaddressed combative colleague).

**Spot the signs.** Friday feels like surfacing for air; a tense body all week; Sunday anxiety; brain fog and missed details; procrastination and self-doubt spirals; harder-to-handle challenges and raw emotions; fewer hobbies and thinner relationships. Ask the person to recall what their early warning signs were last time.

**Purposeful focus.** Name your highest purpose (and your manager's definition). Then split tasks into **primary** (directly tied to core purpose) and **peripheral** (still necessary, but easy to over-serve because they're easier, feel rewarding, or support others). Ask how much of the day goes to each, and what would the CEO say matters most this year? Perfecting documentation or answering every message within five minutes rarely tops the list. The challenge is focus, not effort.

**Set expectations and context.** Communicate what you're prioritizing and why; build an environment (calendar blocks, agreed response times, saying no or deferring) that protects focused time. Consider how scarcity (of time, resources, support) shapes behavior, and reflect on the regret that overwork tends to produce: common end-of-life regrets include working too much, losing touch with friends, and not living a life true to yourself.

**Rewire and rebalance.** Prolonged stress puts you in reactive, lower-brain thinking and makes problem-solving harder. The author's path out: treat commitments to yourself as seriously as commitments to others; **make one small commitment to yourself each day**; rest even when your brain says it's unproductive; a structured program on inner saboteurs; an evening journal with prompts for energy, a restful moment, movement, nourishment, connection, and gratitude; creative hobbies; regular exercise; and using frustration as a cue to thank or cheer on a teammate.

**Focus on alignment and be proactive.** Check whether your environment supports your highest purpose. Use internal and external resources early. If the environment itself keeps producing burnout despite your efforts, that is information, and it connects back to the PRD in `alignment.md`.

**Be careful.** If someone describes severe, persistent symptoms (can't sleep, hopelessness, physical illness), don't coach it as a productivity problem. Respond with care and encourage them to seek professional support.

**Exercises to offer:** What were your early warning signs last time? What share of your week is primary vs. peripheral? What is one small daily commitment you can make to yourself? What expectation do you need to reset, and with whom?
