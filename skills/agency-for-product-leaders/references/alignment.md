# Part I: Alignment

## 1. Self-awareness

Self-awareness means closing the gap between how you see yourself and how you show up for others. It is an ongoing loop of reflecting, getting outside input, and adjusting.

**Three ways in:**
- **Lifeline exercise.** Plot the key events of your life, high and low, then look for patterns in what drives you. Ask what the person was proud of, what hurt, and what themes repeat.
- **Personality assessments** are a lens, not a definition. Use them to ask better questions about strengths and tendencies, not to label yourself.
- **Observe your thoughts for a week.** Prompts: Was my self-talk mostly positive or negative? Would I speak to someone else this way? What recurring themes appear? How did my thoughts affect my confidence, actions, and outcomes? To interrupt a spiral, use future-focused framing ("three things I'm proud of," "three things I'd do again") or a retrospective ("what do I want to start, stop, continue?").

**Collecting feedback well**
Barriers to name: feedback fatigue, fear of judgment, the sting of personal feedback, and uncertainty about how to use it.
1. **Choose constructors.** Seek feedback from people who have experience in the area, know your work well enough to be specific, and give honest feedback meant to help you improve.
2. **Make it easy to give.** Be specific. Compare "How am I doing with sharing data?" with "In yesterday's meeting, how could I have used the data more effectively to build confidence in the idea?" Give a heads-up so people can prepare. Offer the "if you were in my position, what would you do?" frame, which makes people more willing to be candid. Start with gratitude and follow up with curiosity.
3. **Reflect and act.** Ask: What is true for me? Is this a dot, a line, or a trend (one is a dot, two is a line, three is a trend)? If I address it, what could the impact be? Does it fit the leader I want to become? What is one step I can take this week? Keep the action specific, simple, and time-bound.

**Exercise to offer:** Choose one or two areas to understand better over the next month. For each, name the action that will gather insight, a date to revisit, and afterward record what was learned, what will change, and what will continue.

## 2. Values and purpose

Values are what you notice when something feels "off." They give you a reference point for decisions, which reduces second-guessing and regret.

**Four principles for defining values**
1. **Start small.** Three to five values. If the list is long, rank by pairwise comparison and notice which values contain others (courage may express altruism; trust may be a prerequisite for courage).
2. **Embody, don't just admire.** Ask "Do I just admire this quality or do I live it?" Useful probes: What behaviors in others really push my buttons (what repels you reveals what you stand for)? When did I feel proud of how I showed up? Where is the gap between what I value and what I do? Micro-rituals can close gaps (e.g., ending meetings five minutes early to honor consideration).
3. **Make them action-oriented.** Treat values as verbs. Define what each looks like in practice: courage means speaking up when something feels off; clarity means setting direction and expectations early.
4. **Don't outsource them.** Don't borrow someone else's values or "browse" for ones that sound impressive. Test: "If no one were watching, would this still matter to me?"

**Values at work.** Your values won't always match how a company operates. Occasional tension is normal. Persistent conflict leads to dissonance and dissatisfaction. Use the framework to see where you're compromising in ways that don't sit right.

**Worked example to adapt:** A PM is asked to remove a "cancel subscription" button so users must email support (a dark pattern). Rather than silently complying or just objecting, they identify the top reasons users cancel and propose fixing those, so the solution serves both users and the business.

**Clarifying purpose: Skills / Gifts / Passions.** Three columns. *Skills* are abilities developed over time. *Gifts* are natural inclinations that come easily. *Passions* are what sparks energy and curiosity; connect passions to formative events from the lifeline. Look for through-lines. This works best with a trusted facilitator who can reflect patterns back. Purpose then helps with trade-offs between speed and quality, users and business, short term and long term.

## 3. Life-first career design and "your own PRD"

The idea: your career is one part of your life, not the starting point of your identity. Design the career inside the larger picture of values, relationships, interests, and long-term vision. This protects against over-indexing on work and accepting roles that demand sacrifices you never intended.

**Your own PRD** (a play on product requirements), used to build your **catalytic environment**, the conditions where your energy, skills, and impact compound:
- **P, Problems you want to solve.** Which kinds of problems consistently engage you? Consider audience (B2B vs. B2C bring different dynamics: stakeholder complexity and long relationships versus broad user experience, rapid iteration, and segment prioritization), scope, and environment. Don't narrow too fast; keep exploring and talk to PMs in spaces you're curious about.
- **R, Requirements that help you thrive.** Conditions live in two places: the **company** (mission, priorities, structure, what gets rewarded, how decisions are made) and the **people** (who you'll be surrounded by daily; they shape how you think and what you normalize). Define your requirements the way you'd weigh a restaurant or event: who's there, what's the vibe, what's the cost. Examples from the author: impact, growth, psychological safety and respect, trust and autonomy, financial health and transparency. The person's list may differ.
- **D, Developing a dynamic mindset.** Careers unfold through experimentation, feedback, and adjustment, so your PRD is never finished. Notice how you feel at work and after work (energized or drained?). When you find friction, first see if it can be addressed in the role. If misalignment persists after you've raised it, it may be the wrong environment, and "there is such a thing as right and wrong when it comes to choosing a company, and it's defined by you."

**Exercises to offer:** Who's on your invite-only entrance list to your world? Describe your current environment: what supports and what drains? Which problems do you most want to solve? Which requirements are non-negotiable in this season? What trade-offs are you willing to make, and which are you done tolerating? Design one small experiment for the next few weeks.

## 4. Imposter syndrome

Imposter syndrome is a pattern of beliefs about yourself, not a verdict from others. It often shows up as a *feeling* before a thought: discomfort, hesitation, vague unease. Behaviors that look responsible (overpreparing, perfectionism, procrastination, staying quiet, minimizing your work, attributing credit to others) act like a weighted life jacket: protective, but they slow you down when chronic.

**Step 1: Identify your imposter.** Valerie Young's five competence types: Perfectionist (impossibly high standards), Superhero (worth tied to overworking), Natural Genius (things should come easily), Soloist (struggles to ask for help), Expert (must know everything before acting). Naming the pattern makes it visible, but the person does the naming: offer the types as a menu and ask which, if any, feel familiar. Don't assign one from a single incident.

**Step 2: Take action around belief.** Beliefs can be changed because you control them. Practical check when overpreparing: "Am I doing this to avoid judgment or embarrassment, or does this task genuinely need more attention to deliver the value required?" Focus on delivering value over perfection.

**Comparison and survivorship bias.** You see others' outcomes, not their false starts and doubts. (The classic illustration: armor was proposed for the parts of returning WWII bombers that showed damage, until someone realized the missing data was from planes hit elsewhere that never returned.) Compare who you are now with who you're becoming.

**Three factors that fuel self-doubt**
- **External factors.** Bias, stereotypes, favoritism, or highly competitive peers. Use your values; let externalities inform rather than define you; build a network outside work; use your voice and role to improve the environment where you can. You always retain agency, including asking for change within a time frame or choosing to leave.
- **Novelty.** New role, team, company, or product. Create the frame, then break it down. Ask: What is one step I can take today to understand my next step? What do my manager and leaders expect? What steps set me up to succeed? Which tasks come first, and what knowledge gaps must I fill? Ask for context, find an ally, build relationships, drive a tangible outcome.
- **Scope.** Large work causes paralysis. Define clear goals tied to users, the organization, and leadership objectives; match scope to goals (consider a more iterative approach, cut features not crucial for v1); list known steps and open questions; assign owners and check-in times. Take the first concrete step, then the next.

**Step 3: Meet your imposter with compassion.** Self-empathy (observing your baseline without exaggerating shortcomings or dismissing strengths) plus self-compassion (offering yourself the support you'd give a friend). Ask: what would I tell a trusted friend in this situation? Prepare three thoughts to return to when doubt appears, thoughts that lead to action instead of retreat.

## 5. Six tools for an empowered mindset

The author's six practices for keeping agency over how you think and respond, especially under uncertainty or pressure. The aim: hold both vigilance and possibility-seeking, so risks are weighed without being paralyzed by them.

1. **Recognize and redirect your inner critic.** Notice when your thoughts are an engine versus an anchor. Shirzad Chamine's "saboteurs" (Judge, Avoider, Controller, Hyper-Achiever, Hyper-Rational, Hyper-Vigilant, Pleaser, Restless, Stickler, Victim) give useful language, though you don't need the whole framework. Process: recognize the pattern, ask what the consequences are if it keeps driving you, then commit to a specific small action (e.g., put a failing process on the team agenda; block time for the hard problem). For a restless pattern, ask what's pulling you away: overwhelm, avoidance, boredom? Then ask "If I get to the end of today, what will I regret not finishing based on my highest purpose?"
2. **Acknowledgment and gratitude.** They work together; gratitude without honest acknowledgment feels hollow. Example: "I fumbled how I shared the metrics" (acknowledgment), then "I'm glad the first few slides landed, so I'll take that win." Mindfulness means noticing facts without spiraling into a story: what actually happened, what feedback came, what worked, what to improve.
3. **Balance prevention and promotion thinking.** Promotion thinking plays to win (bold bets, quick decisions, creativity, but more error-prone). Prevention thinking plays not to lose (careful, risk-aware, but its successes are invisible). Neither is better; success comes from balance. Identify which you lead with, then decide where to apply each. Under a tight deadline with a proposal to cut UX, apply prevention thinking. When an off-roadmap idea appears, make room for promotion thinking.
4. **Self-regulation.** Reclaim the space between stimulus and response. Chronic stress puts the reactive brain in charge and crowds out complex problem-solving. Your team watches you, so your steadiness shapes the emotional climate. It is not about masking emotion; it is about being heard and respecting people. Five practices: (1) give yourself grace, since the first emotion is normal and the expression is what matters; (2) recognize your reaction is a choice; (3) take a beat using the "hidden pedestrian" idea: before honking at the stalled car, remember there may be context you can't see, and re-engage with curiosity; (4) ask what words and actions you'll be proud of tonight, next week, or months from now; (5) attack issues, not people: be blame-agnostic, name the shared problem, rally the right people.
5. **Create space for creativity.** Without space, thinking shrinks into "four-walled" optimizing within existing boundaries. Creativity needs psychological room to float bad ideas ("judge me for my good ideas, not my bad ones"). Pressures that suppress it include expected evaluation, surveillance, contracting for reward, being told exactly how to do the task, peer competition, and extrinsic motivators; many are normal in high-performing workplaces, so name them and ask for the right support. The author's technique is a **"NO RULES" tool**: generate ideas first (e.g., a mind-map with the problem at the center), then star the promising ones and only afterward reintroduce constraints to test feasibility and trade-offs. It can also be a whiteboard outdoors or floating wild ideas past customer support. Mindfulness (stepping away from the desk, a walk or drive in quiet, writing down the core problem) helps too.
6. **Don't forget to play.** Treat the work like a game: it has goals, challenges, teammates, setbacks, and wins, and playfulness makes ambiguous work more energizing and sustainable. Play can be planned or impromptu: a friendly competition, a light icebreaker, humor as a release valve. It builds trust and psychological safety and invites creativity. The work still matters, and you also decide how seriously to take things. Rules can be made and changed, including the ones you set for how you show up. Serious outside hobbies can also lift confidence at work.

**Exercise to offer:** Which saboteur or imposter type shows up most? What is its trigger, and what is one small redirect you will try this week?
